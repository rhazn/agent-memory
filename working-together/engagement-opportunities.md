# Engagement Opportunities

## Goals and Audience

- Seek organic professional visibility through honest conversation, not reaction farming or disguised sales pitches.
- Prioritize nontechnical founders, company operators, and domain experts who could become Isselthal customers and discuss concrete problems that software can solve, especially in small or medium-sized businesses. Technical peers can be interesting connections, but keep them secondary rather than letting topic fit crowd out potential customers.
- Strongly deprioritize other agency people as an audience: Philip generally does not see them as potential customers. Topic relevance alone does not compensate for that audience mismatch. Do not generalize this to all consultants or product founders; he explicitly liked a process-optimization discussion by a consultant and connecting with a founder building a relevant product.
- Connecting with other product builders is valuable in its own right, including through honest accounts of launches, customer discovery and implementation problems. Do not assess every connection solely as an immediate customer prospect.
- Personal-interest discussions can be worthwhile without a business angle.
- Treat Philip primarily as a software engineer who also builds data pipelines, not primarily as an open-data researcher. Build visibility for his professional services through useful contributions, not pitches.
- A person's company and its relevance to Isselthal can strengthen the fit, but the topic must also be genuinely interesting.
- Seek process improvement and digitalization problems: connecting systems, cleaning data, building data pipelines, and adapting software to domain needs, including laboratories.
- Relevant topics include software architecture, deployment, servers, Kubernetes, open-source adoption, hosting and customization, useful open data sources, and practical AI integration for specific tasks.
- Philip explicitly likes the supporting infrastructure that makes AI useful in practice: running and operating AI, building and maintaining knowledge bases, testing and evaluating AI, and preparing data so AI can use it. Treat this as a broad interest, not just internal documentation maintenance or generic AI promotion.
- Open data, research, open standards, and European collaboration remain topics of interest, but do not default to research-heavy or data-heavy discussions.

## Search Directions

- Cover four distinct directions each run:
  1. Software engineering and operations: architecture, technical debt, self-hosting, digitalization and SME processes.
  2. Data engineering: pipelines, integration, data quality and cleaning.
  3. Research and laboratory data: practical workflows, systems and data management. Include instrument APIs, automation, simulation and integration reliability, not only laboratory data management.
  4. Esports: software engineering, data, analytics, data science and AI. This is a standing direction, supported by Philip's strong and uncommon esports background. Do not restrict it to data workflows.
- Treat these directions as a starting point, not a closed list.
- After the main search, revisit Philip's background and the directions already tried. Test one new direction that could reach people he cares about, even when the main search yields fewer than three suitable posts.
- Present the exploratory direction separately: explain the query, audience and fit, and include its strongest qualifying opportunity with a proposed comment. Do not pad with a weak result if none qualifies; explain the shortfall instead.
- Ask whether Philip wants to keep, tweak or drop the exploratory direction. Maintain the compact direction record below, including queries tried and abstract feedback, so future searches build on prior exploration.

## Post Selection

- Select posts from the past week. Prefer more recent posts.
- Prefer short, ordinary LinkedIn posts and questions over long articles or blog-style posts that require substantial reading before joining the conversation.
- Favor some existing engagement and room for a meaningful exchange. Avoid crowded threads with hundreds of comments and announcements with no discussion or engagement. Do not impose fixed engagement thresholds.
- Avoid engagement bait and authors whose visible activity is predominantly engagement bait.
- Philip is sensitive to formulaic closing questions used as engagement hooks. A closing question alone is not an exclusion: he also values genuine invitations to exchange experience. Assess whether the author has something concrete to discuss and whether the question serves that discussion.
- Give the combined pattern of a dramatic opening hook, a formulaic closing question and packaged hashtag-heavy presentation more weight as a negative style signal, even when the technical subject fits. Philip finds this pattern off-putting and AI-like. It does not prove AI authorship, and no single cue is an automatic exclusion. Substantive working details do not by themselves cancel the style concern.
- Topic relevance is not enough. Look for a substantive discussion rather than a product announcement with little room to respond.
- Honest launch posts can qualify when they explain a real problem, customer discovery or a product-building decision and leave room for a useful exchange. Do not equate launches with engagement bait or require an explicit closing question.
- Favor posts that feel human-written and invite honest engagement. Do not claim to know how a post was written merely from its style.
- Teaching-style posts can be too blog-like even without an external article. Prefer ordinary conversations and concrete problems over polished explanatory posts.
- Exclude posts when the signed-in account cannot comment. If explaining the exclusion, state the actual restriction, such as "only the author's connections can comment," rather than calling it a "restricted discussion."
- Be cautious about topics outside Philip's expertise, especially culturally or politically sensitive discussions involving marginalized communities. Do not suggest confident contributions without sufficient context.

## Comment Voice

- Use plain ASCII punctuation in comment drafts: straight apostrophes (`'`), straight double quotes (`"`), and hyphens (`-`). Never use curly quotes, typographic apostrophes, en dashes, or em dashes. Preserve language-specific letters such as umlauts.
- Use plain, natural language and few specialist terms.
- Avoid formulaic analytical praise such as "the distinction ... is particularly interesting." Philip explicitly dislikes this as AI-sounding prose. Open with a direct reaction, a concrete point or a genuine question instead.
- Write somewhat longer, conversational comments with enough context to develop the point. Do not default to a terse one- or two-sentence question. Add substance, not filler; do not try to demonstrate expertise in every reply or impose a fixed length.
- Favor genuine curiosity, a focused question, a relevant observation, or a supported personal example.
- Do not quote the source post or article in comment drafts. Philip explicitly dislikes that device, especially as an opening; develop an original response instead of echoing a memorable sentence.
- Questions about actual implementation and how people handle judgment calls can make a useful conversational contribution. Do not invent what Philip has read or which tools he is considering. A tool reference or outside link needs a known, relevant reason, not an assumed personal interest.
- Engage primarily with the content of the post. Use author and company research to assess fit, not to make the comment sound like a researched sales approach.
- For unfamiliar authors, avoid pointed, complex questions about their specific business unless the post invites that depth. A straightforward business question can fit, but do not imply prior familiarity.
- Simple questions about actual users and products built with a tool can be more natural than elaborate technical questions. Where supported by Philip's experience, a concrete software-engineering analogy followed by a genuine implementation question also fits. Do not make either a fixed template.
- Use concrete wording that makes the reasoning clear. Avoid compressed or abstract phrases that require the reader to infer the point.
- Allow genuine uncertainty and informal language. Do not replace a natural exchange with polished expert advice.
- Light humor, smileys and modest self-deprecation can fit when natural. Do not turn them into a required style or add them to every draft.
- Bring relevant outside knowledge from software engineering into a domain discussion when it adds a useful analogy or fresh perspective. Do not restate the post in more technical language or present a rule of thumb as a universal law.
- Appreciation and thanks are welcome when sincere.
- Do not default to unsolicited tips or instructions. Share an idea as part of a conversation, not as a drive-by expert intervention.
- Comments should react naturally, not try to elicit engagement. Share relevant knowledge, cross-field connections, supported personal experience, or simple appreciation. Ask a question only when Philip has a genuine question. Do not force comments into a fixed template.
- When Philip lacks domain experience, express honest interest or ask for a follow-up rather than manufacture a specialist contribution. A simple question about an available framework can be sufficient.
- Favor warm, conversational reactions grounded in the actual work. Use familiar examples to explain cross-domain engineering connections. Do not force every comment into an observation-plus-question pattern.
- Never invent experience, opinions, or familiarity.

## Supported Background and Contribution Angles

- Philip's practical AI mindset is to let AI help build tools that produce outputs, rather than ask AI to produce the final outputs directly. For suitable repeatable data transformations, he favors small inspectable scripts, reproducibility and code review over generating results and merely spot-checking them. Do not overstate this: scripts can still contain bugs, and code generation does not guarantee correctness.
- Philip finds fully AI-generated applications less satisfying when he has not explored the approach first. For learning, he favors discussing approaches, mechanisms and the meaning of the data before implementing a chosen approach.
- Philip reports that documentation built for agents can also help educate humans. Reuse of internal AI-facing documentation is a useful side effect, not a solution to unclear responsibility; he thinks AI can make responsibility problems worse.
- Through Morsel, Philip explored improving and maintaining technical documentation. His assessment was that it was hard to develop a compelling sales story because companies often treated documentation as a cost center. He sees internal knowledge bases as a potentially better direction. Use this as his experience and assessment, not as proof of market demand or a claim that Morsel achieved results not recorded here.
- Personal project experience can support candid peer conversation about product positioning and customer problems. Avoid turning that experience into a researched sales pitch or assuming familiarity with the author's company.
- Philip values failing fast, early and loudly rather than continuing in a partially working state. His software-engineering example is failing service startup when a required environment variable is missing, allowing deployment to stop or roll back before the fault goes unnoticed. Use as a concrete contribution when relevant, not a universal rule against recovery.

## Confirmed Learnings

- Cross-domain software-engineering analogies work well for process-improvement discussions when they add a concrete idea rather than restate the post. Independent positive feedback supports connecting hidden human adjustments to accumulated software workarounds, and pairing that comparison with a genuine question about how people discover those adjustments. Light humor can fit but is not required. Do not force this structure onto other topics.

- Omit descriptions of existing replies and statements about what was read. Keep those checks behind the scenes.
- Briefly explain any project, working group, standard, or initiative needed to understand the discussion.
- Base these explanations on the post and relevant linked sources. Read those sources rather than guessing from names.
- Before a proposed comment that refers to a specific incident, term or example, explain that reference immediately above the draft. State what happened and why it matters so Philip can judge the comment without opening the source. Distinguish details in a linked article from those in the short post. A vague mention that an error or suspect commit was misleading is insufficient; explain the reported symptom, actual cause and incorrect lead when the draft depends on them.
- State missing context or access limits clearly.
